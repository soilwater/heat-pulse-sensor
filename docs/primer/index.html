1. The measurement in one page

The sensor has a small circuit board (the "head") and three thin needles that go into the soil. The center needle is a heater: seventeen tiny 3.3 Ω resistors in a row. The two side needles carry thermometers (thermistors TH1 and TH3), 8 mm away from the heater. A third thermistor, TH2, sits at the tip of the heater needle, and TH4 sits on the board to report the board's own temperature.

A data logger asks for each measurement over a single data wire, using SDI-12, a standard way for environmental sensors to talk to loggers. One measurement goes like this (Figure 1 shows where each part sits):

  1. Background (10 s). Record the soil temperature before heating, so we can subtract it later.
  2. Heating (15 s). Push about 2 W through the heater. That is roughly the power of a small flashlight bulb, spread along 44 mm of needle.
  3. Cooling (150 s). Keep recording while the heat spreads outward. The side needles warm up by about half a degree, peak about 23 s after the heater switches off, and slowly cool.
  4. Analysis. How high the side-needle curve rises tells us how much heat the soil can store (its heat capacity). How fast the peak arrives tells us how fast a temperature change spreads through it (its diffusivity). Together they give the thermal conductivity, and the heat capacity gives the water content, because water stores far more heat than soil minerals or air.
Analogy. Drop a spoonful of dye into still water and watch a spot 8 mm away. In thick syrup the color arrives late and stays concentrated; in water it arrives quickly and fades fast. The heat pulse is the dye, the soil is the liquid, and the side thermistor is your eye. The one difference: we know exactly how much "dye" (energy, in joules) we released, because we measure the heater power.

Two pieces of engineering make this work: an electronic circuit that measures temperature to a few thousandths of a degree (Sections 2 and 3), and a physical model that converts the temperature curve into soil properties (Sections 4 to 6).

2. Electricity for soil scientists

2.1 Voltage, current, resistance

Three quantities describe almost everything on this board:

Figure 2 pictures these quantities with water. Handy prefixes: 1 mA = 0.001 A, 1 µA = 0.000001 A, 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω, 1 mW = 0.001 W. Ground (GND) is simply the point we call 0 V; every other voltage on the board is measured from it, just as elevations are measured from sea level.

2.2 Ohm's law and power, with the heater

For a resistor, current is proportional to voltage:

$$ V = I\,R \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad I = \frac{V}{R} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad R = \frac{V}{I}$$

and the power it turns into heat is

$$ P = V\,I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}. $$

Worked example: the heater. Seventeen 3.3 Ω resistors in a row (in series, one after the other) add up: \(R_\text{heater} = 17 \times 3.3 = 56.1\ \Omega\). If the full 12 V battery sat across the heater, the current would be \(12/56.1 = 0.214\) A. In reality a few other things are in the same loop, and each takes its share:

Part of the loopResistance or drop
Input diode D1 (protects against a reversed battery)≈ 0.35 V drop
5 m of 22 AWG cable, out and back (10 m of wire)≈ 0.53 Ω
Current-sense resistor R5 (the INA226 measures the current here)0.10 Ω
Heater switch Q1 (a MOSFET, when on)≈ 0.03 Ω
Board copper in the heater loop≈ 0.20 Ω
Heater, 17 × 3.3 Ω56.10 Ω
$$ I = \frac{12\ \text{V} - 0.35\ \text{V}}{56.10 + 0.53 + 0.10 + 0.03 + 0.20\ \Omega} = \frac{11.65}{56.96} \approx 0.2045\ \text{A} $$

So the heater gets \(P = I^2R = 0.2045^2 \times 56.1 = 2.35\) W while it is on, and each little resistor gets \(0.2045^2 \times 3.3 = 0.138\) W, well under its 0.33 W rating.

PWM: turning a heater "down" with a switch. We cannot easily change the battery voltage, but we can switch the heater on and off very fast. The processor will switch Q1 100 times per second and keeps it on 85% of each cycle (the duty cycle). The soil cannot feel 100 flickers per second, so it sees the average: \(0.85 \times 2.35 = 1.99\) W. Over a 15 s pulse the energy is \(E = P\,t = 1.99 \times 15 \approx 29.9\) J. That energy divided by the heater's effective length, \(29.9\ \text{J} / 0.0442\ \text{m} \approx 677\) J per meter, is the key input to the soil model in Section 5.

2.3 Series resistors and the voltage divider

Put two resistors in series between a voltage \(V_\text{top}\) and ground. The same current flows through both, \(I = V_\text{top}/(R_1+R_2)\), so the voltage at the point between them is

$$ V_\text{mid} = I\,R_2 = V_\text{top}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}. $$

This is the voltage divider, and it is the heart of the temperature measurement: if \(R_2\) changes with temperature, so does \(V_\text{mid}\). If \(R_1 = R_2\), the midpoint sits at exactly half of \(V_\text{top}\).

Analogy. Water flows down a hill through two stretches of narrow pipe. The pressure halfway down depends on how the total "resistance" is split between the upper and lower stretch. Make the lower stretch narrower and more of the pressure drop happens there, so the midpoint pressure rises.

2.4 Capacitors and the RC time constant

A capacitor stores electric charge, like a small bucket. Its size, the capacitance \(C\), is measured in farads (F); ours are 100 nF (0.0000001 F) and 1 µF. The charge it holds is \(Q = C\,V\): a bigger bucket, or a fuller one, holds more.

A capacitor does two useful jobs: it smooths (it absorbs sudden spikes and fills in brief dips, like a water tower on a town's water line), and together with a resistor it sets a delay. If you fill a capacitor through a resistor \(R\) from a voltage \(V_0\), it charges along

$$ V(t) = V_0\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = R\,C. $$

The time constant \(\tau\) is the whole story: after one \(\tau\) the capacitor is 63% full, after 5\(\tau\) it is 99.3% full, and after 10\(\tau\) it is within 0.005% of its final value. Example: 1.5 kΩ and 1 µF give \(\tau = 1500 \times 0.000001 = 1.5\) ms.

Analogy. Filling a bathtub through a garden hose. The tub is the capacitor, the hose is the resistor. A thinner hose (more R) or a bigger tub (more C) means a longer wait. The tub fills fast at first and slows as it nears full, because the pressure difference that drives the flow shrinks. That is the exponential curve above.

3. The thermistor circuit, part by part

3.1 The thermistor

A thermistor is a resistor made to change its resistance with temperature. Ours are "NTC" (negative temperature coefficient): warmer means lower resistance. At 25 °C it is nominally 10 kΩ (±1%). The standard "beta" equation describes it:

$$ R(T) = R_{25}\,\exp\!\left[\beta\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{298.15\ \text{K}}\right)\right], \qquad R_{25} = 10\ \text{k}\Omega,\ \beta = 3380\ \text{K}, $$

with \(T\) in kelvin (°C + 273.15). Near room temperature the resistance changes by about \(\beta/T^2 \approx 3.8\%\) per degree, which is large; that is why thermistors are good at sensing small temperature changes (Figure 4, left).

3.2 Why a reference resistor (R21–R24)?

The processor cannot measure resistance. It can only measure voltage. The reference resistor turns a resistance into a voltage by forming a divider with the thermistor (Section 2.3; Figure 3):

$$ V_\text{node} = V_\text{REF}\,\frac{R_\text{NTC}}{R_\text{ref} + R_\text{NTC}}. $$

Example at 22 °C: \(R_\text{NTC} = 11.22\) kΩ, \(R_\text{ref} = 10\) kΩ, \(V_\text{REF} = 3.53\) V, so the current is \(3.53/21.22\ \text{k}\Omega = 166\ \mu\text{A}\) and \(V_\text{node} = 3.53 \times 11.22/21.22 = 1.87\) V. Warm the soil and the thermistor's resistance drops, so the node voltage drops.

Why 10 kΩ? Choosing the reference equal to the thermistor's nominal value puts the node near half of VREF, where the divider is most sensitive to small changes. Why 0.1% precision and 25 ppm/°C? Any error in the reference resistor looks exactly like a temperature error. A 1% resistor would shift the reading by about 1%/3.8% ≈ 0.26 °C; the 0.1% part keeps that below 0.03 °C, and its low temperature coefficient keeps it from drifting as the board warms or cools. For the soil properties, what matters is the temperature rise, and a fixed offset cancels when the background is subtracted: a 1% reference error changes the rise by only about 0.2%. The thermistor's \(\beta\) value is different: an error in \(\beta\) scales every rise, and therefore \(\lambda\) and \(C\), one for one.

Does the divider linearize the thermistor? Only as a side effect. The right panel of Figure 4 is not an approximation of the left one: it is the exact result, code = 16384 × R / (R + 10 kΩ). The fraction R / (R + Rref) flattens toward 1 when the thermistor resistance is large and toward 0 when it is small, which counteracts the thermistor’s steep exponential curve. The result is an S-shaped curve that is nearly straight around the temperature where the thermistor equals the reference resistor (25 °C here) and bends more toward cold and hot temperatures. The processor never uses a straight-line approximation: it inverts the exact divider and beta equations (Section 3.3), which costs only a few lines of arithmetic. What the divider’s shape buys is nearly even resolution across the range of soil temperatures:

3.3 From voltage to a number: the ADC

The processor's analog-to-digital converter (ADC) compares the input voltage with a reference voltage and reports a whole number, the code. With 14 bits there are \(2^{14} = 16384\) steps:

$$ \text{code} = 16384 \times \frac{V_\text{node}}{V_\text{AREF}}. $$

Here is the trick in this design: the ADC's reference (AREF) is connected to the same VREF that feeds the dividers. Substitute the divider equation and VREF cancels:

$$ \text{code} = 16384 \times \frac{R_\text{NTC}}{R_\text{ref}+R_\text{NTC}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad R_\text{NTC} = R_\text{ref}\,\frac{\text{code}}{16384 - \text{code}}. $$
Analogy: a stretchy ruler. Measure a rubber band with a ruler printed on the same rubber. If both stretch together, the reading does not change. This is called a ratiometric measurement: VREF can sag or drift a little and the temperature reading stays the same, because the "ruler" (AREF) and the "object" (the divider) move together.

Worked example. At 22 °C: code = 16384 × 11.22/21.22 = 8663. Going back: \(R = 10\ \text{k}\Omega \times 8663/(16384-8663) = 11.22\) kΩ, and inverting the beta equation,

$$ T = \left[\frac{\ln(R/R_{25})}{\beta} + \frac{1}{298.15}\right]^{-1} - 273.15 = \left[\frac{\ln 1.122}{3380} + \frac{1}{298.15}\right]^{-1} - 273.15 = 22.0\ ^\circ\text{C}. $$

How fine is the measurement? Near 22 °C the code changes by about 158 codes per °C, so one code is about 0.006 °C. The planned firmware averages 64 readings per sample (the twin assumes this), which reduces random noise further. The signal we want, the side-needle rise, is about 0.5 °C, or roughly 80 codes. The real limits are noise, thermistor calibration and the reference resistor, not the number of steps.

3.4 Why the thermistors are switched on only to read

Current through a thermistor heats it. This self-heating is a real problem when the signal is half a degree. During a reading the thermistor carries 166 µA with 1.87 V across it:

$$ P = V I = 1.87\ \text{V} \times 166\ \mu\text{A} \approx 0.31\ \text{mW}. $$

The maker quotes a "dissipation constant" of about 1 mW/°C in still air, so 0.31 mW would warm the thermistor by about 0.3 °C; in epoxy and soil heat escapes more easily, so the real warming is smaller. Left on all the time, that would be a steady warming comparable to the signal. A perfectly steady offset would cancel when the background is subtracted, but this one would not be steady: it depends on how well the thermistor is coupled to the soil, which changes with moisture, and it warms the very spot being measured. So pin D4 powers the whole thermistor network only 20 ms out of every second (2%). The average heating power drops to about 6 µW, and the self-heating to a few thousandths of a degree. It also saves battery.

Analogy. Checking whether bread is done by touching it: a quick tap tells you the temperature; holding your hand on it warms the bread. The thermistors get a quick tap once per second.

3.5 Why R11 (1.5 kΩ)?

Pin D4 is a processor output, and processor pins are delicate: the RA4M1 data sheet lists 4 mA per pin for this port (Arduino's Nano R4 documentation quotes up to 8 mA; the design uses the more conservative data-sheet value). When D4 switches on, C13 is empty and behaves for an instant like a short circuit to ground. Without a resistor the pin would try to deliver a large current. R11 limits it by Ohm's law:

$$ I_\text{peak} = \frac{4.75\ \text{V}}{1500\ \Omega} = 3.2\ \text{mA} \;<\; 4\ \text{mA}. $$

(An earlier version used 330 Ω, which would have allowed about 14 mA; that is why it was changed.) Once C13 is charged, R11 carries the steady current of the four dividers plus the ADC reference, about 0.8 mA. That current makes a voltage drop across R11, \(1500\ \Omega \times 0.8\ \text{mA} \approx 1.2\) V, which is why VREF settles near 3.5 V rather than 4.75 V. Thanks to the ratiometric trick, that lower value does not matter.

3.6 Why C13 and C21–C24?

C13 (1 µF) holds VREF steady. The ADC draws small, quick sips of current from its reference while it converts. Without a local "bucket," each sip would dent VREF through R11. C13 supplies the sips and refills between them. With R11 it charges with a time constant of about 1.1 ms (R11 in parallel with the divider load, times 1 µF).

C21–C24 (100 nF each) hold each thermistor node steady. Three reasons:

  1. The ADC takes a gulp. Inside the ADC, a tiny capacitor is connected to the input for a moment and must be charged to the input voltage. Through 5 kΩ of divider resistance, that gulp would pull the voltage down briefly. The 100 nF capacitor is thousands of times larger than the ADC's internal one, so it supplies the gulp with almost no change in voltage.
  2. The needle wires are antennas. The thermistor wires run 30 to 55 mm down the needles, right next to a heater that switches 100 times per second. They pick up electrical noise. The capacitor, together with the divider resistance, forms a filter that smooths out fast disturbances while passing the slow temperature signal.
  3. A known, short settling time. Each node settles with \(\tau = (10\ \text{k}\Omega \parallel R_\text{NTC}) \times 100\ \text{nF} \approx 5.3\ \text{k}\Omega \times 100\ \text{nF} = 0.53\) ms. The planned firmware waits 10 ms before reading. That is nearly 19 of the node's own time constants, but the node also follows VREF, which settles more slowly (about 9 of its time constants in 10 ms). The node-to-VREF ratio is within about one ADC step at the first reading and closer on average, and the small remainder is the same every second, so it cancels when the background is subtracted.
Why not bigger capacitors? Bigger capacitors would smooth more but take longer to charge, so pin D4 would have to stay on longer (more self-heating) and the network would need longer to discharge between readings. The values are a balance between steadiness and speed.

5. The line-source model

5.1 The heat equation

Heat flows from warm to cold at a rate proportional to the temperature gradient (Fourier's law), and energy is conserved. Combining the two gives the heat equation:

$$ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\nabla^2 T, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\lambda}{C}. $$

In words: a spot warms up if it is colder than the average of its surroundings, and the rate is set by the diffusivity. For a long, straight heater it is natural to use distance from the heater, \(r\), as the coordinate; if nothing changes along the heater's length, the equation becomes

$$ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\left(\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\right). $$

The extra \(1/r\) term appears because heat spreads over ever-larger rings: a ring twice as far away has twice the circumference to warm.

5.2 Assumptions and boundary conditions

To solve the equation we must say what the world looks like (Figure 8). The classic dual-probe analysis (Campbell et al. 1991, who treated the heat pulse as instantaneous; Kluitenberg et al. 1993 and Bristow et al. 1994, who included its finite duration) assumes:

  • Geometry: the heater is an infinitely long, infinitely thin line.
  • Medium: the soil is uniform (homogeneous and the same in every direction) and extends forever in every direction.
  • Initial condition: everything starts at the same temperature; we work with the rise \(\Delta T = T - T_0\), which starts at zero everywhere.
  • Far-field boundary condition: far away the soil never feels the heater, \(\Delta T \to 0\) as \(r \to \infty\).
  • Source condition: the line releases a constant power per meter, \(q'\), from time 0 to time \(t_0\), then stops.
  • Everything else is ignored: the needles' own size and heat storage, the epoxy, contact resistance between needle and soil, the soil surface, water moving in response to heat, and properties changing with temperature.

5.3 Continuous and pulsed line source

If the line heats forever, the solution (Carslaw and Jaeger 1959; Blackwell 1954) is

$$ \Delta T(r,t) = \frac{q'}{4\pi\lambda}\,E_1\!\left(\frac{r^2}{4\alpha t}\right), $$

where \(E_1\) is the exponential integral, \(E_1(x) = \int_x^\infty \frac{e^{-u}}{u}\,du\). You do not need to compute it by hand: it is built into scientific libraries (for example scipy.special.exp1 in Python or expint in MATLAB), but not into common spreadsheets or data-logger function libraries; Knight and Kluitenberg (2004) give a simple polynomial approximation for those cases. All you need to know is that it is large for small arguments (long times, short distances) and falls quickly to zero for large ones. The group \(r^2/(4\alpha t)\) is a natural "clock": the heat front reaches a distance \(r\) at a time of order \(r^2/(4\alpha)\), which for our 8 mm and a typical soil is about 30 s.

Switching off: superposition. The heat equation is linear, so solutions can be added. Turning the heater off at \(t_0\) is the same as leaving it on and adding a second, negative heater that starts at \(t_0\):

$$ \Delta T(r,t) = \frac{q'}{4\pi\lambda}\left[E_1\!\left(\frac{r^2}{4\alpha t}\right) - E_1\!\left(\frac{r^2}{4\alpha (t - t_0)}\right)\right], \qquad t > t_0. $$

Figure 9 shows how the two pieces combine.

Analogy. Stopping a faucet is equivalent to leaving it running and opening a drain of the same size underneath. Mathematically this is simpler, because both the faucet and the drain have the same, known behavior.

5.4 Reading soil properties from the peak

The side-needle curve rises, peaks at a time \(t_m\) with height \(\Delta T_m\), and falls. Setting the time derivative of the pulsed solution to zero gives a formula for the diffusivity from the peak time alone (Bristow et al. 1994):

$$ \alpha = \frac{r^2}{4}\;\frac{\dfrac{1}{t_m - t_0} - \dfrac{1}{t_m}}{\ln\!\left(\dfrac{t_m}{t_m - t_0}\right)}. $$

Then the peak height gives the conductivity, and the ratio gives the heat capacity:

$$ \lambda = \frac{q'}{4\pi\,\Delta T_m}\left[E_1\!\left(\frac{r^2}{4\alpha t_m}\right) - E_1\!\left(\frac{r^2}{4\alpha (t_m - t_0)}\right)\right], \qquad C = \frac{\lambda}{\alpha}. $$
The intuition, from a very short pulse. If all the energy \(Q' = q' t_0\) (joules per meter) were released at once, the solution would be \(\Delta T = \frac{Q'}{4\pi\lambda t}\exp\!\left(-\frac{r^2}{4\alpha t}\right)\); its peak comes at \(t_m = r^2/(4\alpha)\) and has height (Campbell et al. 1991)
$$ \Delta T_m = \frac{Q'}{e\,\pi\,r^2\,C} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad C = \frac{Q'}{e\,\pi\,r^2\,\Delta T_m}. $$

Read it like this: the pulse of energy spreads into a cylinder of soil of roughly radius \(r\) (cross-section \(\pi r^2\)); the temperature rise is energy divided by (volume × heat capacity), and the factor \(1/e\) (with \(e \approx 2.718\)) is how far down the bell-shaped temperature profile the thermistor sits at that moment: at \(t_m\) the profile has \(\exp(-r^2/4\alpha t_m) = e^{-1}\) at distance \(r\). A soil that stores more heat (wetter) shows a smaller peak. The formula also shows why the spacing matters so much: \(C\) depends on \(r^2\), so a 1% error in spacing becomes a 2% error in heat capacity.

Why 44.2 mm and not 43.2 mm? Measured from the first resistor's outer end to the last one's, the 17 resistors span 43.2 mm. But each resistor stands for one 2.6 mm stretch of an evenly heated line: seventeen stretches of 2.6 mm make 44.2 mm. That is the length the line-source model needs. Using 43.2 mm overstates \(q'\) by 2.3%, and \(\lambda\) and \(C\) come out 2.3% high (Section 6.3).

Now pretend we measured that curve and want the soil back. With \(t_m = 38\) s, \(t_0 = 15\) s and \(r = 0.008\) m:

$$ \alpha = \frac{(0.008)^2}{4}\cdot\frac{\tfrac{1}{23} - \tfrac{1}{38}}{\ln(38/23)} = 1.6\times10^{-5}\cdot\frac{0.01716}{0.5021} = 5.47\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-1}. $$

The two \(E_1\) arguments are \(r^2/(4\alpha t_m) = 0.770\) and \(r^2/(4\alpha(t_m - t_0)) = 1.272\), with \(E_1\) values 0.3281 and 0.1415:

$$ \lambda = \frac{45.11}{4\pi \times 0.5221}\,(0.3281 - 0.1415) = 1.283\ \text{W m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}, \qquad C = \frac{1.283}{5.47\times10^{-7}} = 2.345\ \text{MJ m}^{-3}\text{K}^{-1}. $$

We recover the soil to within rounding, as we should when the data follow the model perfectly. The short-pulse shortcut gives \(C = 677/(2.718 \times \pi \times 0.008^2 \times 0.5221) = 2.37\) MJ m⁻³ K⁻¹, only 1% off, a good sanity check you can do on a calculator.

How sensitive is this? Two small errors, worked out for this example:
  • Peak time off by 1 s (37 or 39 s instead of 38 s): \(\alpha\) and \(\lambda\) change by about ±3.5%, but \(C\) changes by less than 0.1%. The peak time sets the diffusivity; the conductivity follows it, and the heat capacity, which depends mostly on the peak height, hardly moves.
  • Spacing off by 0.1 mm (analysis assumes 8.1 mm, needles really 8.0 mm): \(\alpha\) comes out 2.5% high and \(C\) 2.5% low; \(\lambda\) does not change at all, because both \(E_1\) arguments stay the same. In general \(C \propto 1/r^2\), so a spacing error of \(\varepsilon\) percent gives about \(2\varepsilon\) percent in \(C\). This is why needle spacing is the most important dimension of the sensor, and why bent needles matter (Kluitenberg et al. 1993; Kluitenberg et al. 1995).
Instead of using only the peak, one can fit the pulsed solution to the whole curve by nonlinear least squares, which uses every data point; Bristow et al. (1995) compared these approaches. The twin fits the curve, using the one-second TH1 readings from heater-on to 1.5 times the peak time (Section 7.6).

5.6 From heat capacity to water content

Heat capacity adds up by volume (de Vries 1963):

$$ C = \rho_b\,c_s + \theta\,C_w \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \theta = \frac{C - \rho_b\,c_s}{C_w}, $$

with bulk density \(\rho_b\) (Mg m⁻³), mineral specific heat \(c_s \approx 0.75\) MJ Mg⁻¹ K⁻¹, water \(C_w = 4.18\) MJ m⁻³ K⁻¹ and volumetric water content \(\theta\) (m³ m⁻³). Air adds essentially nothing. For the Hodgeman core, \(\rho_b = 1.363\):

$$ \theta = \frac{2.345 - 1.363 \times 0.75}{4.18} = \frac{2.345 - 1.022}{4.18} = 0.32\ \text{m}^3\,\text{m}^{-3}. $$

The core's measured water content is 0.39. The gap shows that the de Vries relation has its own scatter (organic matter, mineral mix, the KD2 reading itself); in the Kansas dataset's wetter states (saturated, 33 and 70 kPa), measured heat capacities average about 6–9% below this line, with a scatter of about ±11–14%, and drier states scatter more. This core sits 12% below it (2.345 measured against 2.665 predicted). A sensor-specific calibration against measured water contents would remove much of this.

6. The digital twin's model

6.1 Seventeen point sources instead of a line

The real heater is not an infinite line. It is made of 17 separate resistors 2.6 mm apart (a 44.2 mm effective length), and it starts about 9 mm below the board. To predict what the real sensor would see, the twin treats each resistor as a point source in uniform soil. For a point releasing power \(P\) from time 0, the rise at distance \(d\) is (Carslaw and Jaeger 1959)

$$ \Delta T(d,t) = \frac{P}{4\pi\lambda\,d}\;\text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{d}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right), $$

where erfc is the complementary error function (another standard function). The twin switches each source off at \(t_0\) by superposition, as in Section 5.3, and adds up all 17:

$$ \Delta T(r,z,t) = \sum_{i=1}^{17} \frac{P_i}{4\pi\lambda\,d_i}\left[\text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{d_i}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right) - \text{erfc}\!\left(\frac{d_i}{2\sqrt{\alpha (t-t_0)}}\right)\right], \qquad d_i = \sqrt{r^2 + (z - z_i)^2}, $$

with the second term only after \(t_0\), \(P_i = 1.994/17 = 0.117\) W, and \(z_i = 8.8 + 2.6\,(i-1)\) mm the position of resistor \(i\) along the needle. TH1 and TH3 sit at \(z = 29.8\) mm, the middle of the heated section, and \(r = 8\) mm. TH2 sits on the heater needle, 3.68 mm past the last resistor. Figure 10 shows the resulting temperature field.

6.2 "Grid" resolution, time steps and boundary conditions in the twin

Many soil models divide the soil into a grid and step forward in time (finite differences or finite elements), and then grid spacing and time step control the accuracy. The twin has no grid. Both the line-source and the point-source solutions are exact formulas: the temperature at any point and time is computed directly, independently of every other point. So there is no numerical diffusion and no stability limit. What the twin does choose:

6.3 Why the twin's estimate differs from the soil values

The twin runs the measurement in two directions, like a real experiment:

  1. Forward (the "truth"): the 17-point-source soil of Section 6.1, plus the electronics: 14-bit codes, averaging, one sample per second, subtracting the background.
  2. Inverse (the "analysis"): what simple firmware would do: fit the ideal pulsed line source (Section 5.3) to TH1's rise, with the nominal 8 mm spacing.

Two analysis choices decide how close the answer comes. Take first a simple analysis that divides the power by the resistor-end-to-end length, 43.2 mm, and fits the whole 105 s record of a 90 s cooling phase. For the Hodgeman soil it reports \(\lambda = 1.331\) W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ (+3.7%) and \(C = 2.406\) MJ m⁻³ K⁻¹ (+2.6%). A real sensor adds more differences that the twin deliberately leaves out, because they need measurements to quantify: the needles' own radius and heat capacity, which shift and rescale the peak (earlier and higher, or later and lower, depending on whether the needles store less or more heat per volume than the soil; Knight et al. 2012); epoxy, contact resistance, and air gaps around the needles; the exact spacing after assembly and insertion; heater resistance changing slightly as it warms; and the soil not being uniform. That is why heat-pulse sensors are usually calibrated: measure a material with known properties (water stabilized with agar is common), and adjust the effective spacing until the sensor reads correctly. That absorbs a constant bias in \(C\), including the heated-length bookkeeping above, but not in \(\lambda\): in the line-source analysis \(\lambda\) does not depend on \(r\) at all. Getting \(\lambda\) right needs the correct \(q'\), or a second check against a material of known conductivity (water, about 0.6 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹).

And the "true" soil values are measurements too. The reference values come from a KD2 Pro SH-1, a commercial dual-needle probe with its own geometry, processing and uncertainty. Agreement within a few percent between two different instruments is good.

6.4 A real KD2 Pro curve

The Kansas dataset stores the raw temperature rise that the KD2 Pro recorded for each reading. The SH-1 probe has two 30 mm needles 6 mm apart and heated this core for 60 s at 18.3 W m⁻¹.

Figure 12 shows the curve. One point first: the KD2 computed its reported \(\lambda\) and \(C\) from this same curve with its own analysis, which we do not know in detail. So any gap between those values and our fit is first a difference between two analyses of identical data. With that in mind, here is what the curve teaches, with numbers checked by simulation:

  • The finite length matters more with a long pulse on a short probe. Simulate the SH-1 geometry (30 mm heater, 6 mm spacing, 60 s at 18.3 W m⁻¹) in the reported soil and fit the ideal line to that noise-free, finite-heater curve over the same 118 s: the fit returns \(\lambda = 1.407\) (+9.7%) and \(C = 2.342\) (−0.1%). The heat-capacity estimate is almost untouched; the conductivity is not.
  • Finite length does not explain everything. Fitting the ideal line to the measured curve gives \(\lambda \approx 1.56\) and \(C \approx 2.27\). The finite length accounts for about half of the conductivity difference and none of the heat-capacity difference. The rest comes from things neither ideal model includes, such as the needles' own radius and heat capacity (Knight et al. 2012), contact between needle and soil, and the instrument's own processing; this one curve cannot separate them.
  • The peak comes only 4–6 s after the heater stops, and the readings are 2 s apart, so single-point peak methods are fragile here: moving \(t_m\) from 64 s to 62 or 66 s moves \(\alpha\) by +67% or −25%.

7.2 Two rules of thumb you can do on a calculator

From the short-pulse solution (Section 5.4), treating the pulse as if it were released all at once at its midpoint:

$$ t_m \approx \frac{r^2}{4\alpha} + \frac{t_0}{2}, \qquad \Delta T_m \approx \frac{Q'}{e\,\pi\,r^2\,C}, \qquad Q' = q'\,t_0. $$

These are approximations. They work within a few percent while the pulse is short compared with \(r^2/(4\alpha)\), and fail for long pulses (Section 7.4). The comparisons below use the full pulsed line-source solution of Section 5.3, which is what the rules approximate. Check them on the present design (medium soil, \(r\) = 8 mm, \(t_0\) = 15 s, \(Q'\) = 677 J m⁻¹):

$$ \frac{r^2}{4\alpha} = \frac{(0.008)^2}{4 \times 5.47\times10^{-7}} = 29.2\ \text{s} \;\Rightarrow\; t_m \approx 29.2 + 7.5 = 36.7\ \text{s} \quad(\text{full solution: } 38.0\ \text{s}), $$ $$ \Delta T_m \approx \frac{677}{2.718 \times \pi \times (0.008)^2 \times 2.345\times10^{6}} = \frac{677}{1282} = 0.528\ ^\circ\text{C} \quad(\text{full solution: } 0.522\ ^\circ\text{C}). $$

7.3 What if the needles were 6 or 10 mm apart?

By hand. Only \(r\) changes. \(r^2/(4\alpha)\) becomes 16.5 s at 6 mm and 45.7 s at 10 mm, so \(t_m \approx\) 24.0 s and 53.2 s (full solution: 26.0 s and 54.0 s). At 6 mm the time rule is 8% low, because the 15 s pulse is nearly as long as \(r^2/(4\alpha)\). The peak scales with \(1/r^2\): \(677/(2.718\,\pi \times 0.006^2 \times 2.345\times10^6) = 0.939\) °C at 6 mm and 0.338 °C at 10 mm (full solution: 0.909 and 0.336 °C).

What changes.

  • Signal: 6 mm gives about 1.7 times today's peak, 10 mm about 0.64 times.
  • Sensitivity to spacing error: since \(C \propto 1/r^2\), a 0.1 mm error costs \(1-(r/(r+0.1))^2\): 3.3% at 6 mm, 2.5% at 8 mm, 2.0% at 10 mm. Closer needles need tighter spacing tolerance.
  • Model error: with the heater unchanged, a wider gap makes the heater "shorter" relative to the distance heat must travel, so the end effect grows: at 10 mm the fitted \(\lambda\) is +0.8% to +1.6% and \(C\) +0.4% to +0.6%; at 6 mm both stay within 0.1%.
  • Not in this model: the needles themselves (2.77 mm outside diameter for 12 gauge) take up a larger share of a 6 mm gap, so the effects of needle radius and heat capacity that the ideal model ignores (Knight et al. 2012) become more important as the gap shrinks. Our 12-gauge needles are already thicker than the roughly 1.3 mm needles of typical research probes, so those effects are likely larger here than in most published sensors.

7.4 What if we heat for 8, 15 or 60 seconds?

The Kansas readings in this chapter were taken with a KD2 Pro SH-1, which heats for 60 s (Section 6.4). Today's dual-probe sensors typically heat for 8–15 s; ours defaults to 15 s, and 60 s remains available only as a setting.

By hand. The power per meter stays 45.11 W m⁻¹, so the energy is \(Q' = 45.11 \times t_0\): 361, 677 and 2706 J m⁻¹. For 8 s: \(t_m \approx 29.2 + 4 = 33.2\) s and \(\Delta T_m \approx 361/1282 = 0.282\) °C (full solution: 33.6 s and 0.281 °C). For 60 s the rules give 59.2 s and 2.11 °C, but the full solution gives 74.3 s and 1.867 °C: the rules of thumb fail. The time rule even puts the peak before the heater switches off at 60 s. A 60 s pulse lasts longer than the 29 s the heat needs to reach the thermistor, so it no longer behaves like a single burst. Use the full equations of Section 5.4.

What changes.

  • Signal: grows with the energy, a little less than proportionally for long pulses: 8 s gives 44 ADC steps in the medium soil, 15 s gives 83, and 60 s (four times the energy of 15 s) gives 295, 3.6 times as many.
  • Heating of the soil next to the needle: the soil touching the heater needle warms much more than the soil at the thermistors. Beside a resistor (between resistors it is about 10% less, and the model ignores the needle wall, which would even this out): in the medium soil 5.2 °C (8 s), 6.8 °C (15 s) and 10.5 °C (60 s); in the dry soil 14.4, 21.3 and 39.5 °C. Strong local heating can push water away from the heater, which changes the very property being measured; this model does not include it, so large local rises are a warning sign, not a computed error.
  • Model error: small for 8 and 15 s. For 60 s it grows in high-diffusivity soil (+5.7% in \(\lambda\), −0.1% in \(C\) for the wet sandy soil), because by the time of the peak the heat has spread well past the ends of the heater.
  • Time and battery: the energy drawn from the battery is proportional to \(t_0\), and a longer pulse delays the peak and the end of the measurement.

7.5 What if the needles were 3 inches long?

A 3-inch (76 mm) needle has room for a 65 mm heater: 25 resistors at 2.6 mm, heater midpoint about 40 mm below the board (the side thermistors would move there too). The catch is the 12 V supply: 25 × 3.3 Ω = 82.5 Ω would cut the power to about 1.4 W. Keeping about 2 W needs about the same total resistance as now, so 25 × 2.2 Ω = 55 Ω. By Ohm's law (Section 2.2) that gives 2.03 W average, spread over 65 mm: \(q' = 31.27\) W m⁻¹, 31% less than now.

  • Signal: the peak falls from 0.522 to 0.362 °C in the medium soil (83 to 57 ADC steps). Heating 22 s instead of 15 s would restore it.
  • Model error: essentially zero in all three soils. But with the effective length and a fit to 1.5 times the peak time, today's 44 mm heater is already within +0.5% in \(\lambda\) and +0.1% in \(C\) (worst of the three soils), so the gain is about half a percent.
  • Bending: a needle held at one end bends in proportion to its free length cubed for a sideways force at the tip (to the fourth power for a force spread along it). The side needles, which set the spacing, now end about 32 mm below the board; moving the thermistors to the new heater midpoint would make them about 1.3–1.5 times longer, so they would bend roughly 2–4 times more for the same force during insertion. Because a 0.1 mm change in spacing already costs 2.5% in \(C\), this is likely to cost more accuracy than the longer heater gains.

7.6 What if we fit more, or less, of the curve?

This question matters more than the needle length. The peak's height and timing carry most of the information: the height mainly sets \(C\), the timing sets \(\alpha\). The tail is where the ideal line and the real sensor agree least. For the finite heater, the curve matches the ideal line through the peak and departs from it only late in the tail (Figure 11, right). The effects the model leaves out, such as the needles' own heat capacity, imperfect contact, heat left from a previous pulse and the soil's natural temperature drift, also show up mostly in the tail. So the further past the peak we fit, the more the fit is pulled by the part of the curve the ideal line gets wrong. This agrees with advice from G. J. Kluitenberg (personal communication, May 2026): fitting the tail, the part of least agreement, is a common mistake, and the fit should end just past the peak.

  • Needle spacing is the biggest practical lever: every 0.1 mm of spacing error costs about 2.5% in \(C\), more than any model error above. Measuring or calibrating each sensor's effective spacing matters more than lengthening the needles.
  • Longer needles remove the remaining model error but reduce the signal by about 30% and bend more.
  • Longer pulses buy signal at the cost of strong heating next to the needle; 15 s is a reasonable middle.

Why can the peak come 96 s after a 15 s pulse? The heating time only shifts the peak by about half the pulse (Section 7.2). The peak time is set mainly by \(r^2/(4\alpha)\): how long heat needs to cross the gap. Oven-dried soil has the lowest diffusivity in the Kansas data (\(\alpha \approx 0.18\) mm² s⁻¹, because air-filled pores conduct heat poorly), so heat needs \(0.008^2/(4 \times 0.18\times10^{-6}) \approx 88\) s to cross 8 mm, and the peak arrives near 96 s. Oven-dried cores are the extreme; field soils are rarely that dry, and most peaks come within 30–65 s.

When to change them (numbers from the same ideal model; confirm on the bench):

  • Wet or sandy soils (high diffusivity). The peak comes early (about 20–25 s), so a shorter cooling phase (60–90 s) is enough. Wet soils give the smallest peaks, so a longer pulse helps the signal; in the wet sandy soil 20 s gives a 0.79 °C peak with +0.8% in \(\lambda\), and 30 s gives 1.12 °C with +1.7% (Section 7.4). Averaging repeated measurements is the other way to beat noise.
  • Dry soils (low diffusivity). The peak comes late (90–100 s in oven-dry soil), so keep the 150 s cooling phase. The signal is large, but the soil next to the heater gets hot: about 21 °C at 15 s in the oven-dry example. Lowering the duty to 50% keeps a 0.54 °C peak and brings that down to about 12.5 °C; do not lengthen the pulse.
  • How often to measure. Each pulse leaves heat behind that fades slowly, and the analysis subtracts a flat background. If the next measurement starts while the needles are still cooling, the falling background biases the result.
